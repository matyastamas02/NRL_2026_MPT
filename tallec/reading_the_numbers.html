Reading the numbers

Every player in four competitions gets a rating out of 100, and any rating can be translated into what it would be worth in another competition. This is what those numbers mean, how far to trust them, and where they stop.

A player is followed by his permanent Stats Perform ID, so the same man is one player whether he turns out in the Queensland Cup, the NRL or Super League. That is what makes the translation possible at all.

How to read a player's rating

One number, three flavours, and a deliberate reluctance to believe small samples.

50 is average — for his position, in his competition

Not average for rugby league. Average for props in Super League, or halves in the Queensland Cup. A prop is measured against props, so his 90 run metres are never held against a fullback's 150. That is the whole point of the scale: it answers "how good is he at his job", not "who ran furthest".

Class, Form and Divergence

  • Class is his level over everything he has played — six or seven seasons where we have them. This is the recruitment number.
  • Form is the same calculation over his last five matches. This is the selection number.
  • Divergence is Form minus Class: is he playing above or below his own standard right now?

What divergence looks like in practice

Jensen Taumoepeau (NSW Cup, 57 matches) has a Class of 42.6 and a Form of 60.6. Over five seasons he has been a below-average NSW Cup player; over the last five matches he has been a clearly above-average one.

Whether that is a 24-year-old improving or a good month is exactly the judgement the system cannot make for you — but it can tell you where to look.

Why a two-game player never tops the table

A player with one enormous game is not a great player; he is a player we know almost nothing about. So every rating is pulled toward the average of his peer group, by an amount that depends on how much evidence there is:

  1. Two matchesWe keep 29% of his own numbers and fill the rest with "average for his position". His rating barely moves off 50.
  2. Ten matchesWe keep about two thirds. The rating starts to be his.
  3. Thirty matchesWe keep about 85% of it. This is a number you can take to a board meeting.

Every rating carries the match count and a confidence flag next to it, and the benchmark tables have a minimum-matches filter set to five by default. If a name you expect to see is missing, raise or lower that filter first.

The competition ladder

The part that took the longest and is the most useful: four competitions measured against each other on one scale.

The question a club actually asks is not "how good is he in the Queensland Cup" but "what is he worth to us". Answering that needs to know how much harder one competition is than another — and that is measured, not assumed.

How it was measured: some players turn out in two competitions in the same season — a fringe NRL squad member playing NSW Cup between call-ups — and others move between them from one year to the next. That gives the same player, at the same point in his career, rated in two places. Whatever differs is the competition, not the player. 953 such moves stand behind the ladder below.

Why we believe the ladder

Two checks that nothing in the method forces to pass. Both directions of every pair agree: players moving NRL to Super League gain about the same amount as players moving the other way lose — measured on completely different sets of players. And the three steps add up: the Queensland Cup to the NRL, plus the NRL to Super League, equals the Queensland Cup to Super League measured directly, to within a tenth of a point.

The one thing that will get us into trouble

Two very different uncertainties sit behind every translated number, and they must not be quoted as one.

Please read this before quoting a translation

The average shift between two competitions is known tightly — to about a point and a half. Across 126 observed moves, an NRL player really does gain about four points in Super League. That number is solid, and it is fine to say so.

An individual player's outcome is not — about fourteen points. That range is as wide as the gap between an average player and a very good one. How a specific man adapts to a new country, a new club and a new style is mostly not predictable from his statistics.

So the honest sentence is: "players making this move typically rate three to four points lower, and this one starts from 72." The dishonest one is "he will be a 68." The first survives a signing that does not work out; the second does not.

Used that way the ladder does real work: it sets expectations, sizes a gap, and stops a club paying NRL money for a Queensland Cup level. What it cannot do is rank two players three points apart and tell you which will settle better.

Contribution, and the team-list simulator

A different question from "how good is he": how much of his team's work actually goes through him.

Contribution is a player's share of his own team's output in a match — run metres, post-contact metres, tackles, line breaks, tries — blended and scaled so the median contributor sits at 50. It is deliberately not a quality rating. A moderate player in a weak side can carry a large share of it.

That makes it the right input for one specific question: what does a team lose when a player is out? The app's squad screen lets you untick names from a squad and shows the expected contribution the line-up loses. That figure is what feeds the match model as an availability signal.

What we learned by testing it against the match model

Adding player-level information to the match model does improve it — by about a quarter of a point of margin error, which is real but small. The interesting part is which player information helps: not who is in form, but how many of the seventeen have barely played. The team's rating already knows how good the team is; what it cannot know is that three of them are debutants.

Where the numbers stop

Worth knowing before anyone leans on them in a meeting.

  • NRL 2026 positions are estimated, not read off a team sheet. Every other competition-season has real per-match positions. For the current NRL season the position comes from the player's other seasons, so a player who has changed role this year may be measured against the wrong peer group.
  • The feeder competitions stop at 2025. NSW Cup and Queensland Cup ratings describe last season, not this one.
  • The translation is measured on players who actually moved. Someone promoted to the NRL was picked for a reason, so the shift measured on him need not apply to a player nobody has promoted.
  • Scores have not been checked against official results. The data is internally consistent — the points in every row add up correctly — which is not the same as verified. Worth a pass before any of it goes near a betting number.
  • A rating is a summary of what happened, not a scouting report. It knows nothing about attitude, injury history, or whether a player can kick under pressure in November.

What would move this forward fastest

Three things, in order of how much they would change.

  1. NRL 2026 positions, the way the Super League master camePosition joined row by row rather than pasted alongside. That is the last remaining gap in the position data, and it would put the current NRL season on the same footing as everything else.
  2. Sign-off on the metric dictionaryAll 341 rows are still marked for review. Ten are flagged low confidence, and one — Charge Down — divides by something that is not in the data. Nothing in the Volume and Rate layer can be finalised until those denominators are agreed.
  3. Team lists, even manuallyEverything about availability is currently measured on the seventeen who actually played, which is only known afterwards. Friday-morning team sheets would turn it into something a club can use during the week.
