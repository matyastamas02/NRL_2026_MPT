Four leagues, one scale

A season of Australian player data arrived this morning. By the end of the day it had answered a question the project had been carrying since July: how much of a player's standing survives a move between competitions. This is what came in, what we did with it, and how the numbers are actually built.

What arrived

One file, 100 MB, sixteen sheets of Stats Perform player-match exports.

Every row is one player in one match, with 340 columns of detail — run metres, tackles, offloads, penalties, time in possession, and a few hundred more. Sixteen sheets cover three Australian competitions across six seasons.

Two things about the shape of it mattered more than the size. 339 of the 340 columns are identical across all sixteen sheets — for a multi-season, multi-competition export, that is unusually clean. And the one column that varies is the one we needed most: sheets that carry Position do not carry Opta Live, and vice versa. Same underlying data, two different export templates.

The file is history, not an update: it stops at 2025. The database already held NRL 2026 and Super League 2021–2026, so the two are complementary — together they make the NRL a continuous 2020–2026 record.

Do we trust it?

Four checks before anything was loaded. Three passed outright; one found sixteen rows that cannot be true.

passed
The scoring adds up on every single row

For all 85,675 rows, Points Scored equals exactly 4·tries + 2·conversions + 2·penalty goals + 1·one-point field goals + 2·two-point field goals. This is the arithmetic a Super League feed failed earlier in the project, where a missing penalty goal flipped which team won a match.

passed
Squads and minutes look like rugby league

Mean 17.0 players per team-match, which is exactly the number a rugby league team names. Mean 1,039 team-minutes against a theoretical 1,040 — thirteen players on the field for eighty minutes. Numbers that arbitrary landing that close is a good sign the export is complete.

passed
Every row is a player who actually played

No zero-minute rows anywhere. 840 rows exceed eighty minutes, up to 94 — extra time and golden point, so per-minute rates stay correct, but any logic that assumes a player's share of eighty minutes would be wrong.

16 rows
Three players are in two places at once

Every row should be unique on player, competition, season and round. Sixteen are not. Blake Moore is recorded playing 80 minutes for Central Queensland and 80 minutes for Sunshine Coast in the same round, six times over, and one of his rounds is an exact duplicate row. Either the export duplicated him, or one Player ID covers two different people.

Those sixteen rows are 0.02% of the file. We did not guess at a fix — the right correction depends on which of the two causes applies, and that is a question for whoever produced the export. They were excluded from the load, kept in an excluded_rows table, and written up with full detail in a separate workbook to send back.

One check we have not done, and should say so plainly: nobody has compared these final scores against official results. The file is internally consistent, which is not the same as correct.

Two things the file gave us for free

Neither was in the brief. Both removed a blocker we had been working around for a month.

The player IDs are global

Each row carries a permanent Player ID, populated on 100% of rows in every sheet. That alone was welcome — names are not safe keys, and several sheets contain more distinct IDs than distinct names, meaning two different players share a name.

The useful surprise was that the IDs are not Australia-only. Of the 137 Super League 2026 players who also appear in this Australian file, 133 carry an identical ID. The numbering is global across Stats Perform, so a player can be followed from Queensland Cup to the NRL to Super League without ever matching on a name.

Earlier loads had keyed some rows by a name-slug, because their source files had no ID column at all. Those were re-keyed to the permanent ID — 513 keys, 67 of them confirmed by a matching date of birth, and 13 candidate matches rejected because the dates of birth disagreed. Which is the point of checking: the same name really does turn up on two different people. NRL 2025 and NRL 2026 now share 364 players under one ID, where before the re-key they shared none — and without that, a "player rating built over years" is impossible.

Position is a per-match fact, and it back-fills

Position had been the standing gap: the earlier feed had no position column, so a prop was being compared against a fullback. The nine sheets that carry Position turned out to record something better than we expected — not a player's general role, but his jersey for that match. The column totals prove it:

From a stat line to a rating

The part that is easy to hand-wave. Here it is on one real player, one real match, with every number shown.

Joseph Tapine played 54 minutes at lock in round 1 of the 2026 NRL season. Here is how those raw counts become a single performance number, and then a rating.

Convert to a rate, so minutes stop mattering

A player who makes 27 tackles in 54 minutes and one who makes 27 in 80 are not the same. Everything is divided by minutes played. Stats where less is better — errors — get their sign flipped, so a higher number is always better.

Compare him against his own position, not the league

A prop's 90 run metres and a fullback's 150 are not comparable. Each rate is measured against the average and spread for that position group, giving a z-score: how many standard deviations above or below a typical back-rower he was. Tapine ran 0.98 metres per minute against a back-row average of 1.40, so that is 0.60 below par. He threw 2 offloads, where a back-rower averages 0.0096 per minute — 1.68 above par.

Blend the nine z-scores using position-specific weights

Tackling matters more for a back-rower than try assists do. Each position group has its own weight vector, summing to one. For the back row, tackles carry 24%, run metres 19%, post-contact metres and offloads 14% each, and try assists nothing at all.

Average his season — then distrust it in proportion to how little we have

Across his 18 ratable matches Tapine averages +0.565. But a season average is still a noisy estimate, and the fewer matches, the noisier. The engine measures two things from the whole league: how much a player bounces around game to game (σ² = 0.159) and how much players genuinely differ from each other (τ² = 0.066). Those two give the fraction of a player's own average we should believe:

B = τ² / (τ² + σ²/n) = 0.066 / (0.066 + 0.159/18) = 0.881

So we keep 88% of Tapine's own number and pull the remaining 12% toward the league average. For a player with one match, B drops to 0.29 — he is dragged 71% of the way back to average, because one match is mostly luck. This is the honest answer to "you cannot rate a player on two games": the engine agrees, and says so numerically.

Put it on a 0–100 scale where 50 is average

Tapine's shrunk rating is +0.497, which maps to 69 out of 100. The scale is a normal curve, so 50 is exactly the middle of his competition and the spread of real ratings runs from about 23 to 80.

The position column, and how to tell whether it is real

Super League had no position data at all. Then it had some. Then it had the wrong kind. The order those arrived in is the most instructive thing in this project so far.

First, a rule for when partial data is worse than none

Because the identifiers are global, position back-filled onto Super League — but only for players who had also played in Australia, which was 37% of the pool. The instinct is that partial data beats none. It does not.

The rating compares a player against the average and spread of his position group. If 37% of positions are known, the other 63% land in one bucket whose "average" is a blend of every position on the field. Two players with identical performances then get different ratings depending on whether we happen to know their position, and "50 = the average of your peer group" stops being true. Precision for a few costs comparability for everyone.

The rule

A competition is rated within position groups only when at least 90% of its pool has a known position; below that it is rated against the whole competition. Which mode applied is written onto every rating row, and the app states it in plain words rather than leaving the reader to assume.

Then the data arrived, and the first version of it was broken

A Super League export with a position column landed the next morning. Loading it took the competition to 100% coverage and flipped it to position-relative — and then a check came back at 12% agreement with what we had independently estimated for those same players from their Australian careers. The same estimate rule, applied within the Australian data, agrees with itself 68% of the time. A gap that size means either the estimate is worthless or the new file is wrong.

Minutes is the test a rugby person can read at a glance. Props play about 46 minutes and wingers about 78 — forwards are rotated, outside backs are not. In the broken file every position averaged 62 to 66 minutes, which is what you get when labels are not attached to the players they describe. The mechanical cause was visible in the file itself: it carried 5,459 position values for 4,476 player rows, 983 of them with no row at all. A per-match column cannot be longer than the data. The column had been placed alongside the rows rather than joined to them.

One player made it concrete. Cooper Jenkins is recorded as a prop through three NSW Cup seasons, a Queensland Cup season and Super League 2025 — 34 minutes a game, 20 tackles. The broken file called him a fullback in 2026, with his stat line unchanged. Same identifier, same date of birth, same job; only the label moved.

That load was rolled back, verified row for row against a backup, and the specific defect reported rather than worked around.

The corrected file passes, and it passes convincingly

The re-export clears every test, and the last row of that table is the one worth dwelling on: the English match sheet and the independent Australian career record agree on 74% of the same players' positions — a higher rate than the Australian data agrees with itself across seasons (68%). Two sources with nothing in common but the player converge.

So Super League is now rated within position groups on 4,472 match-sheet positions, the first 2026 data in the project with a genuine per-match source. Every position group averages between 46.5 and 51.9 on the 0–100 scale, so no position is advantaged. And the demo club now has a full squad: all 321 of Leeds' player-match rows carry a position, where 134 did before.

What this did to the reliability number

Super League's single-game reliability fell from 0.44 to 0.20 when it went position-relative. That is the expected signature, not a regression: standardizing within position removes the between-position differences from what the rating treats as signal, so the engine stops being "reliable" about the fact that props are props. The shrinkage absorbs it — at ten matches a player still keeps about 72% of his own number.

And the fallback turned out to be stronger than advertised

Before real labels existed, a classifier trained on 24,514 Australian match-sheet rows was the fallback for imputing position from the stat profile. Measured against the Australian data it scored 85% on a held-out season; measured across leagues against career-derived labels it scored 76%, which was reported as a lower bound because those reference labels could be stale.

The corrected file makes the real measurement possible, and the lower bound was very conservative. Against genuine Super League match-sheet labels the same classifier scores 86.1% per match and 91.2% per player — 93.5% for players with five or more matches, and 96.0% on the coarse forwards / spine / outside-backs split. A model that has only ever seen Australian rugby league identifies positions in the English game with 86% accuracy per match: the statistical signature of a position travels between hemispheres nearly intact.

Measuring the gap between competitions

The question the project exists to answer, and the reason today's file mattered.

A club looking at a Queensland Cup forward wants to know what he would be worth in Super League. Until today the model answering that was fitted on sixteen hand-collected transfers with a hand-assigned target. The obvious problem: comparing the average NRL player to the average Super League player tells you about the two populations, not about the step between them.

The trick: hold the player fixed

Some players play in two competitions in the same season — a fringe NRL squad member turning out in the NSW Cup between call-ups. That gives two ratings for one player, at one point in his career, each measured against a different pool. Whatever differs between the two numbers is the competitions, not the player.

One observation, in full

Ben Talty, 2025. Thirteen NSW Cup matches rated 61 out of 100 against NSW Cup. Nine NRL matches the same year rated 54 out of 100 against the NRL. Same player, same season, same form — 6.7 rating points lower once he is measured against NRL company.

He is not a special case. Across all 654 same-season pairs 72% of players rated lower in the NRL, and the average across every pair is a drop of 6.3 points.

The file yields 654 of these same-season pairs across 404 players, plus 301 adjacent-season moves between Australia and Super League for another 185 players — matched on name and date of birth, so namesakes cannot slip in. Nine hundred and fifty-five observed moves, against sixteen before.

The ladder

The single number a Super League club asks for falls out of this: an NRL player moving to Super League rates about 3.5 points higher, because Super League is the slightly weaker pool. A NSW or Queensland Cup player moving up to the NRL drops about 6 points.

Why we believe the ladder

Every pair reverses correctly. If Super League is the weaker pool, players moving into it should gain and players leaving it should lose — and they do, measured on entirely different sets of players in each direction.

The three steps add up. Going feeder → NRL costs 0.171 in the underlying units and NRL → Super League gains 0.090, so feeder → Super League should cost about 0.081. It measures 0.048 — a gap of 0.033, roughly one rating point, on standard errors of 0.02 to 0.03. Fit all eight measurements to a single one-dimensional ladder and the goodness of fit comes out at χ²/dof = 1.02: the disagreements between the eight numbers are exactly the size their own error bars predict. Four competitions really do sit on one scale.

The distinction that keeps this defensible

Take Erin Clark, rated 73 in the NRL across 17 matches. The model puts him at 76 in Super League. Two very different uncertainties sit behind that:

The average shift for this move is known tightly: ±1.0 points. Across 126 observed moves, the typical NRL player really does gain about three points.

This individual player's outcome is not: ±11.7 points. That band is about as wide as the entire spread of player ratings, because how one specific player adapts is mostly not predictable from his numbers.

So the ladder is for setting expectations, not for ranking recruits. Presenting the two bands as one number would be the easiest way to oversell this model, and the fastest way to lose a club's trust the first time a signing doesn't work out.

Two more limits worth stating. The players in this sample moved — somebody chose to promote them — so a shift measured on them need not apply to a player nobody has promoted. And the Super League pairs sit a season apart, so a year of ageing and form drift is baked into each one.

Putting it in front of a scout

A rating nobody can read is not a deliverable. Three things the app was saying were no longer true once the data widened underneath it.

It claimed a position ranking it was not doing

The benchmarks screen offered "top second rows, rated against other second rows". Neither half of that held. The engine rates by position group — second row and lock are both back row, half back and five-eighth are both halves — so a list headed "second rows" was drawn from a pool that also contained locks. And at the time Super League was not rated by position at all, so for that competition the claim was simply wrong.

Both are now honest. Grouping follows the engine, and the page reads rating_basis off each rating row and says which applies, rather than leaving the reader to assume: a green banner where the ratings really are within position group, an amber one where fifty means average for the whole competition and the position tabs are descriptive only. Super League has since earned the green one; the wording did not have to change when it did, because it was reading the data rather than asserting a state. The same logic drives the player profile, where the shrinkage note used to promise a "positional average" regardless.

Also worth saying out loud

At this point not one position in the 2026 pool came from a match sheet — the current-season feeds had no position column, so every position was inferred from the player's other seasons via his permanent ID. The page states that. An earlier version of the check hid it, because the condition only fired when coverage was between zero and one, and zero is exactly the case that needs saying.

One match was enough to top the table

Every table sorted by score with no floor on matches played. The top-rated NRL player was Apa Twidle, on a single game. The shrinkage was doing its job — he keeps only 29% of his own number and is flagged medium confidence — but an extreme single game still clears seventeen honest ones when you sort by score.

There is now a minimum-matches control, defaulting to five, applied to every table on the page, and it says how many players it is holding back and why. At five matches the NRL pool goes from 457 rated players to 367, and the leaderboard becomes Max King (12 matches), Keano Kini (16) and Naufahu Whyte (17) — names with enough evidence behind them to defend in a recruitment meeting.

And it would have hung in front of the client

This one was self-inflicted, earlier in the same day. A helper added to fetch each player's age read date of birth with a correlated subquery over the raw table — 122,343 rows across 342 columns, with no index on the key it was matching. Two of the twelve page-and-league combinations never finished inside ninety seconds.

Date of birth now lives on the players registry instead: 3,323 rows, populated for every player, plus indexes on the keys every page filters by. The full run went from two timeouts to eight seconds for all twelve combinations.

How that was caught

Not by clicking through the app. Streamlit ships a headless test harness, so all six pages are now driven for both leagues in one command — twelve combinations, each asserted to raise no exception and to render its tables. That is what surfaced the timeout, and it is what will surface the next one.

Does the player layer improve the match model?

The question the whole player project has to answer eventually, with a baseline that was set in advance: beat the existing match model on the same fixtures, or the complexity is not worth carrying.

The match model predicts a margin from team-level signals — rolling team form and an ELO rating. The player layer adds what those cannot see: who is actually on the field. Both masters store the model's own margin prediction for every fixture, so the benchmark is the shipped model rather than something reconstructed for the occasion.

How the features are built, and why they cannot cheat

Each player-match gets its composite score, and then each player's pre-match rating is built from his earlier matches only: Class is the running mean of every prior performance, Form the mean of his last five. Those are averaged over the players who took the field, weighted by minutes, and differenced between the two sides.

That the features contain no knowledge of the match they describe is tested rather than asserted. Permuting the performance column across all rows and rebuilding drops the correlation between the real and permuted line-up difference to +0.04. The features carry information about players; destroying the performance data destroys them, without touching a single row key.

The standardization is walk-forward as well: each season is scored against the seasons before it, so not even the population a match is compared against comes from its own season. Making that switch is what uncovered a data problem underneath — post-contact metres are only recorded from 2025 onwards, and they were carrying a quarter of a prop's weight in seasons that never measured them. The engine now drops stats a season did not record and redistributes their weight. The published ratings moved by 0.36 points on average when that was fixed, which is less than expected, and the match-model result did not move at all.

The useful signal is inexperience, not form. The ELO rating already knows how good a team is; what it cannot know is that three of the seventeen have barely played. Form adds essentially nothing on top of team-level form — which stops being surprising once you notice that team form is itself an aggregate of these same players. So the recommendation is to wire the player layer in as a rotation-and-debutant signal, not as a "who is hot" signal.

No model needed for the sanity check: fixtures where one side held the top-5% line-up advantage finished at an average margin of +11.2, and the bottom 5% at −8.2. The signal is plainly there. But the line-up difference correlates +0.275 with the margin and only +0.074 with what the existing model gets wrong — the team-level model has already absorbed most of it, which is exactly why the incremental gain is a third of a point rather than three.

The limit that bounds all of the above

Every number here uses the line-up that actually played, which is known only afterwards. On a Friday you know the named seventeen, not who finished the match. So this measures whether line-up knowledge could help — the necessary first question — and not yet what a team-sheet feed would deliver in practice. There is no team-list feed today; that is the next dependency, not a modelling problem.

Giving every competition a rating, and every player his history

Two gaps that were ours to close rather than anyone else's to send.

The feeder competitions were loaded but unrated

NSW Cup and Queensland Cup had been sitting in the database as translation evidence and nothing else — 45,619 player-match rows nobody could search. They are now rated in their own pools, both position-relative: 496 NSW Cup and 492 Queensland Cup players, alongside 466 NRL and 416 Super League. All four are selectable in the app, which meant teaching it that competitions do not share a current season — the Australian feeder data stops at 2025 while the NRL and Super League run into 2026.

A player did not meet his own past

Class is supposed to be a player's long-run level, but it was being computed from one season. Fixing that exposed something worse in Super League: its 2021–2025 rows were keyed by name-slug, because those source files had no ID column, while its 2026 rows carry the permanent identifier. A Super League player's history therefore sat under two different identities and never joined — his Class came from a median of 11 matches while an NRL player's came from 58.

Both sides carry a name and a date of birth within the same competition, so the link is a name-and-date match, taken only where it is unambiguous on both sides. 271 players linked, 16,661 rows re-keyed, and 271 duplicate identities collapsed out of the registry. Super League now has 22,207 rows under permanent identifiers across six seasons, and the median Class sample went from 11 matches to 36.

One decision worth recording

A multi-season Class needs every season measured the same way. Super League's 2026 is position-relative and its earlier seasons are not, so averaging across them would be meaningless. The standardization mode is therefore chosen once per competition from its overall coverage and applied to the whole history — Super League competition-relative throughout at 17%, the three Australian competitions position-relative. Each match is still standardized against its own season's pool, so a strong 2023 is measured against 2023.

The translation ladder was refitted afterwards and came back identical to the last decimal, which is the right outcome: the merge changed who a player is across seasons, and the ladder is measured within seasons.

The last piece: Super League's own history

A six-season master arrived with a position on every row. It changed more than the coverage number.

Every season passed the four tests — tackles per minute spreading 10x, passes per minute 29x with hookers at the top, minutes running 38 to 79, about 1.5 positions per player. Including 2021, which the file's own README said had no position export; the README was written before that season was matched, and the data is real. Checked one step further: the 2021 positions agree with the independent Australian career record on 77.8% of players exactly and 88.9% by position group — better than the Australian data agrees with itself across seasons.

Super League now carries 31,752 match-sheet positions across six seasons, and all four competitions are rated within position group.

And the sixteen rows are closed

The rows held out at the start had a simple answer once the provider looked: two of the three identifiers were each covering two different players. There are two Blake Moores and two James Walshes, and the identifier had been following the more prominent one. The remaining pair — two identical rows for the same club in the same round — was a mis-typed name on a team sheet; one of them is a different player entirely.

So the split covers every row of those identifiers, not only the sixteen: 4 Falcons rows and 21 Redcliffe rows moved to new identities, and all 16 held-out rows are now loaded. The new identifiers are deliberately not numeric — a made-up number could one day collide with a real Stats Perform ID, whereas syn-blake-moore-2 cannot and says what it is. Zero same-player-same-club-same-round duplicates remain.

A judgement call that got graded

Two days earlier, Super League's pre-2026 history had been linked to its 2026 identities by matching name and date of birth, because those files carried no ID column — 271 players, on a method chosen under uncertainty. The new master supplies the real identifiers for every season, so all 271 could be checked against ground truth. 271 of 271 were correct.

What is still open

  • NRL 2026 positions. The roles have reversed. Super League now has match-sheet positions for the current season; the NRL does not — its 2026 positions are estimated, 3,033 rows from a metadata file and 1,754 from players' Australian careers. The same corrected export for the NRL file would close it, and the four tests above are now the checklist for the day it arrives.
  • A team-list feed. The player layer is measured on line-ups known after the fact. Whether the gain survives on Friday-morning team sheets is untested, and that is the only version a club can act on.
  • Regenerate the Super League margin predictions out-of-sample — both to make the player-layer comparison possible there, and because the app reports its accuracy from that column.
  • The metric dictionary. All 341 rows still sit at Decision=Review: ten are flagged low confidence, and one points at a denominator that is not in the data. The whole Volume + Rate layer rests on that sign-off.
  • 2026 for the feeder competitions. The Australian history file stops at 2025, so the NSW and Queensland Cups are blind to the current season.
